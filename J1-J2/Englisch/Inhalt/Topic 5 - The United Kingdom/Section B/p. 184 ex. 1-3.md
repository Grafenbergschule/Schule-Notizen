## Video

**Origins and Arrival** Māori ancestors originally arrived from a northeastern homeland called Hawaiki, led by the navigator Kupe. Upon sighting the land appearing through a long white cloud, they named it Aotearoa, which translates to "Land of the Long White Cloud."

**The Haka** The haka is an expressive ceremonial dance passing down history, ancestral stories, and identity. Originally used in battle, it has become a global symbol of New Zealand through sports teams like the All Blacks. It also serves as an accessible entry point for people to learn the Māori language and integrate cultural traditions into broader society.

**Treaty of Waitangi** Signed on February 6, 1840, at Waitangi between Māori chiefs and British representatives, this document was intended to unite the nation and protect Māori rights following conflict with European arrivals. However, differences in translation between the English and Māori texts created long-standing disputes regarding sovereignty and ownership.

**Tā Moko** Tā moko is the traditional practice of tattooing that serves as a visual record of an individual's identity, genealogy, social status, and tribal connections. Artists craft each design based on a personal conversation with the recipient rather than using pre-drawn templates. Traditionally, patterns were carved into the skin using bone chisels rather than modern needles.

**Connection to Nature** Māori culture holds deep respect for the natural environment, viewing land, forests, and waterways as living entities with a lifeforce. Natural elements are treated with reverence, and activities like swimming in ancestral rivers are seen as ways to achieve physical, mental, and spiritual cleansing.

**Language Revitalization** Te Reo Māori was historically an oral language preserved through song, storytelling, and wood carving before missionary work helped adapt it into a written form. After laws suppressed the language and banned it in schools for an entire generation, major modern revival efforts—including university courses, funding, and broader social acceptance—are underway to ensure its preservation as the foundation of Māori identity.
